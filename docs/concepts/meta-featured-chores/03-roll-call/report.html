Clean-sheet lane · started from a pain, not a product

Roll Call: the home inventory
you already took.

After a fire, flood or storm takes a home, the insurer pays for the contents only against a list of every item. Almost nobody has that list. Their phones and their Facebook and Instagram albums have years of pictures of every room. Roll Call turns those pictures into the claim list, and every line points at the photo that proves it.

Who it is for: households that lost the contents of their home and must itemize them to get paid
Six months of listing from memory becomes one evening of saying yes or no to what the photos already show.
40%of a California total-loss household's contents limit is paid only against an itemized inventory. The other 60% is advanced without one (SB 495, from 2026)S
51%of surveyed 2025 Los Angeles fire survivors said the itemization was difficult, the problem they named most oftenS
≈$45model cost to read 5,000 household photos, at the Meta API's published prices (SAM 3.1: $2.50 per 1,000 images)SA
What changesA survivor reviews instead of remembering. Items they would have forgotten are on the list, each with its photo and its last-seen date, so the money left off the claim shrinks.A
Why Meta, why nowSAM 3.1 finds every instance of a named thing in an image and keeps each object's identity through videoS. Muse Spark reads each crop nativelyS. Muse Glimmer can sort a private photo library on the household's own PCS.
What it never doesIt never generates an image, never claims an item without evidence or the person's own word, and never sends anything to an insurer without a signed, single-use approval.
Position

01The people and the pain

The lane says to start from a real pain. We looked for a population where a long, expensive job is blocked by a capability that models only recently gained. The household that has just lost everything fits better than any office chore. The job is listing hundreds or thousands of possessions, with brand, age and price. The evidence for it is pictures nobody has ever looked at with this question in mind.

How manyFigureMark

What the pain costs

An item that is not on the list usually goes unpaid, even when the insurer agrees the household owned it.Claim-guide wordingS. The list is the gate to the money, and grief makes the list hard to write.
An Eaton Fire survivor, six months later, was still completing "the inventory of every single thing" he and his wife lost, from heirlooms to clothes. He said the process felt like it needed emotional counselling.LAist, six months after the January fires (paraphrase of the reported account)S

California's Insurance Commissioner called the every-item requirement "inhumane"S. After the 2025 fires the state pushed insurers to advance at least 75% of contents limits without an inventoryS. SB 495 then made 60% the floor (capped at $350,000) for total losses in a declared emergency, with no inventory required for at least 100 daysS. The law moves the deadline back. It does not remove the job: the remaining 40%, and everything outside California, is still paid line by line.

02The bet, the target, the kill line

The bet: an ordinary household's photo archive already contains most of its home's contents, and Meta's segmentation and vision models can find, count and de-duplicate them well enough that a survivor reviews a list instead of writing one.
Measurable target (pilot, A)

≥ 60% of the items a household confirms it owned appear on Roll Call's draft without being typed.

≤ 5% of draft lines rejected by the household as wrong, duplicate or not theirs.

≤ 6 hours of review per 1,000 accepted lines, against weeks or months by handS.

≥ 25% more claimed contents value than the same household's memory-only list, made first.

Kill line (A)

On 10 pilot households (2 staged homes with a hand-made ground-truth inventory, plus 8 volunteers who lost contents and lend their archive):

if recall of confirmed items stays below 40% after the memory walk, or rejected lines exceed 15%, or the added value over a memory-only list is under 10%, then photo archives do not contain the home. We stop, and we keep only the pre-loss "Ready" mode (§ Slice).

The ≈$45 cost, worked out. All of it is assumption (A) on published prices (S). Take 5,000 home photos. SAM 3.1 at $2.50 per 1,000 images is $12.50, and 2,400 video frames at $0.20 per 1,000 is $0.48S. Muse Spark reads about 25,000 crops at about 300 input and 80 output tokens each: 7.5M in and 2M out at $1.25 and $4.25 per million is about $18S. Muse Wonder settles hard merges, about 2M in and 0.2M out at $4 and $20 per million, so about $12S. Tokens per image crop are unverified. Nobody on the team has called the Meta API yet.

Machine

03How it works

There are six parts, each with its own diagram below. Pictures move left to right. Private sorting happens on the household's side of the line, and only photos of the home's interior cross to the Meta Model API. The household's yes or no drives every step from part 4 on.

The data model

An item is a claim about the world, so it must carry its evidence. These are the four tables everything else reads from. The markdown-on-disk form follows Athena's brain (§ Reuse).

-- one picture or video the household gave us; write-once
sighting_source(id 'src_…', kind photo|video|listing|shared, taken_at, sha256,
                inside_home bool, room_id, decided_by glimmer|user)

-- one instance of a thing, in one frame, with SAM's mask
sighting(id 'sig_…', source_id, frame_ms, concept 'chair', mask_rle, box,
         track_id -- SAM 3.1 video identity, null for stills,
         attrs_json -- Muse Spark: brand, model, material, colour, condition)

-- one possession; must cite ≥1 sighting, receipt or memory statement
item(id 'itm_…', room_id, label, qty, qty_basis co_visible|receipt|user,
     first_seen, last_seen, status candidate|accepted|edited|rejected|retired,
     evidence_tier receipt|photo|memory, sources [sig_…|rcpt_…|mem_…])

-- what it costs to replace; always an estimate, always cited
valuation(item_id, rcv_usd, basis receipt|comparable|user, comparable_url, seen_on, note)
Machine · part 1

1Gather and sort: find the home inside the life

Most photos in a camera roll show people, food and trips. The pipeline first has to decide which pictures were taken inside this home, and it does that before any of them leave the household. The household confirms about twenty "anchor" photos of its own rooms. Muse Glimmer, a 30B open-weight model that runs on a single consumer GPUS, then labels the rest as "inside this home", "elsewhere" or "unsure". It also tags each photo with a room. Exact duplicates are dropped by hash, and EXIF dates and places help where they existA.

Machine · part 2

2See and count: every lamp, not just the subject

A person looking at a birthday photo sees the child. Roll Call needs the bookshelf, the lamp and the four chairs behind them. SAM 3 "exhaustively segment[s] all instances of an open-vocabulary concept specified by a short text phrase"S. SAM 3.1 adds faster joint multi-object tracking in video ("Object Multiplex")S. On the Meta API, one call returns detections, masks and identity-preserving video tracksS press. We prompt it with a fixed list of about 300 household concepts per room type, so a kitchen gets "stand mixer", "knife block", "dutch oven" and so on. Each mask becomes a crop. Muse Spark, natively multimodal and reported strong on entity recognitionS, describes each crop's brand, model, material and condition as structured outputS.

Machine · part 3 · the hard problem

3Merge across years: one sofa, 312 sightings

The hard problem is not seeing objects. It is knowing which sightings are the same object, and whether that object was still in the house on the day of the loss.

Eight years of photos show the same sofa hundreds of times. The same archive shows two identical lamps that are two items, and a television replaced in 2021 that must not be claimed twice. The merge has three steps. (a) Within one video, SAM 3.1 track ids already join the sightingsS. (b) Across stills, crops are grouped by room, concept and Muse Spark's attributes, then by visual similarityA. (c) Only the uncertain pairs, typically a few hundred, go to Muse Wonder with every crop of both candidates at once. Its 1M-token context fits a whole cluster in one callS. It answers same | different | can't tell with the crop ids that decided it. "Can't tell" becomes a question for the household, never a guess.

Why this is hard, and why we name it: a wrong merge loses an item, and a missed merge double-claims one. The second is worse, because it looks like fraud. So the threshold is set so that duplicates are rarer than misses, and the household's review in part 6 shows similar items side by sideA. The recall bench in § Slice measures both error rates against a hand-made ground-truth inventory.

Machine · part 4

4Prove and price: three kinds of evidence, one rule

An item enters the schedule only if it cites at least one piece of evidence. There are three kinds, ranked from strongest to weakest. A receipt proves ownership and price: with the household's consent, Roll Call searches the mailbox for order confirmations through Athena's existing read-only Gmail connector (search_mail, reversible: true, side_effects: none). A photo proves presence and gives a last-seen date. The household's own spoken word (part 5) is accepted, and labelled as such. Replacement cost always comes with its basis: the receipt, a dated comparable listing with its URL, or the household's own figure.

Valuation stays deliberately modest in the slice: replacement-cost estimates with a cited basis, which the household can edit. Depreciation to actual cash value follows each policy's own rules and is post-hackathon work (§ Slice).

Machine · part 5

5Remember aloud: a walk through the house

Photos miss the inside of drawers, closets and the garage. So the last step is a spoken walk through each room, and the photos lead it. Muse Spark knows what the photos showed in the kitchen. It asks about what photos rarely show: "Photos show a stand mixer on this counter in 2023. What lived in the cupboard below it?" Muse Voice Transcribe, on the Meta API at $0.18 per hour of audioS, turns the answers into text. Each answer becomes a "memory" line that cites the recording. The household can stop at any time, and the coverage map shows which rooms are thin.

Machine · part 6

6Attest and export: a sworn list goes out once

A proof of loss is signed by the policyholder, and Roll Call treats it that way. Review happens room by room. The household sees cropped items, not the full family photo, unless they ask to see it. That choice is trauma-aware: a survivor should not have to scroll through a lost living room to approve a lampA. A finished room is attested on one card. The export, a spreadsheet in the insurer's columns plus an evidence binder with each line's crop, mask outline, photo date and receipt, is a gated action under Athena's single-use approval.

05Athena Portable: a library of proven parts, not the product

The lane says to look at Athena only after choosing the problem. Its product, an agent operating web apps in desktop tabs, does not belong here. A survivor does not need an agent in their invoicing tool. Its trust core does belong: the rules that a memory must cite its source, that an irreversible step runs once and only when signed, and that every model call is costed. Those are exactly the rules a sworn inventory needs. Every path below was read on disk at athena-portable/.

What reuse saves

About 11,400 lines of tested Python in the kept and changed modules (core 5,881, connectors 2,780, docread 1,375, plus harness and peer files; wc -l on disk). Most of all, the trust properties have already been attacked: 306 hostile turns, zero breaches across three runs (measured on Nemotron and the team's CLI engine, runs 20261007T140609Z, …205627Z, …213007Z).

What it does not save

Everything that makes Roll Call useful is new: sorting, segmentation, merging, valuation and the walk. None of Athena's numbers says anything about Meta models or about inventories. They show only that the gate and provenance core hold under attack.

Argument

10What round 2 should prototype

1 · The item card

One possession with its sighting timeline, crops across years, evidence tier, value basis, and the "retired?" question. This is where the hard problem becomes trust. If this card reads clearly, the product works.

2 · The room review

Accepting or rejecting 150 kitchen lines in ten minutes, shown as crops, never the full family photo, with similar items side by side. It must be fast and gentle. Test it with people, not fixtures.

3 · The binder an adjuster opens

The export as the other side sees it: one page per room, each line with its crop, mask outline, date and receipt. Round 2 should test whether a claims professional would accept it faster than a spreadsheet, since that is what decides adoption.
